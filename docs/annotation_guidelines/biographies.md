# Annotation guideline: Biographies

**Read `general.md` first**: its rules on who "I", "you",
"it" and "he" refer to apply here and win where the two differ.

The worked examples below come from a trial draw and are **not** in the annotation packet. Examples with
an item id (*003*, *044*) are pilot rows, already adjudicated.

## The unit and the question

- **Unit:** one atomic fact, generated by OpenFActScore's fact generator from a model's
  biography of a person.
- **Question:** *Is this fact about the person supported by the reference source?*
- **Shown:** the person's name, then their Wikipedia article (the same `enwiki-20230401` index
  the verifier retrieved from). The model's full response is **not** shown, on purpose: judge
  the atom as written, not what the model may have meant.

Read the atom with the person as its subject: "He", "She", "They" and "The person" refer to
the person named at the top. **"I", "we" and "you" never do**: they are the model or its reader,
even when the rest of the atom fits the person (`general.md` rules 1 and 4). An atom built on
one of them is edge case 1.

## What each label means

| Label | Use it when |
|---|---|
| `supported` | The article states it, or it follows directly from what the article states. Paraphrase is fine. |
| `hallucinated` | The atom makes a claim about the person and the article does **not** support it: the article contradicts it or any specific part of it (a wrong date, role, place, title or relation), **or the article never mentions it**. |
| `unverifiable` | Only two cases: the atom is not a factual claim about the person (edge case 1), or the person's article is missing from the packet. Never "I could not verify it": an atom the article does not mention is `hallucinated`. |

**The rule is the verifier's rule.** OpenFActScore asks whether the atom is "True or False
based on the given context", so an atom the article never mentions comes out False. We label
the same way, so that κ measures whether the verifier got it right, not the gap between two
definitions. The cost is that a true fact the article skips counts as hallucinated; that is
FActScore's own trade-off, and the paper reports it as such.

**Judge against the article only**. If you know the atom is true or false from
elsewhere, say so in `notes`, but label it by the article. Write one line in `notes` for any
call you hesitated on.

**The `tag` column**. Three edge cases below also take a tag: exactly `not a claim`
(edge case 1), `no article` (edge case 3) or `exaggerated` (edge case 4). Leave `tag` blank
otherwise, and never put a tag in `notes` instead: the agreement script reads tags from `tag`
only, and stops on any other value there. `notes` stays free text, so you can add a reason
next to a tag.

## Worked examples

**1. Clear supported.** *Rebecca Romijn*: "Her role is as Mystique."
Article: "She is known for her role as Mystique in the original trilogy (2000–2006) of the
'X-Men' film series." → **`supported`**.

**2. Clear hallucination.** *Francesco Mimbelli*: "He was a painter."
Article: "Francesco Mimbelli (16 April 1903 … – 26 January 1978 …) was an Italian naval
officer who fought in World War II." The article never mentions painting →
**`hallucinated`**. (This holds even for a short stub that never says what he did.)

**3. Hard: partly right.** *Chris John (boxer)*: "Christopher Austin John is a former WBO
featherweight champion."
Article: "Yohannes Christian 'Chris' John … held the WBA (Super) featherweight title from 2004
to 2013." "Former featherweight champion" is right; the full name and the sanctioning body
(WBO, not WBA) are wrong → **`hallucinated`**. **Any unsupported specific makes the whole
atom hallucinated**, even if the rest is right.

## Edge cases

**Edge case 1: the atom is not a factual claim about the person.** Base
models often drift off topic, and the fact generator turns whatever they wrote into atoms.
Examples from the trial draw: "I asked him a question." "Gulshan Kumar is a fact." "What is
her sign?." "The Digital Millennium Copyright Act was enacted in a year." "Creating a profile
is similar to creating profiles on other dating sites."
→ **`unverifiable`**, with tag **`not a claim`**. The agreement script finds these rows by the
tag and reports κ both with and without them.
**Run the four-step test at the top of `general.md` before deciding an atom is unsure.** It
settles almost every case. **Unresolved references are settled, not doubts**. An atom whose subject is "I",
"we" or "you", or an "It" that does not refer to the person, is `not a claim`. From the pilot:
*003* ("I" and "you" are the model and its reader), *001* and *020* ("It" is not the person),
*041* ("I" works as a model, which fits Elsa Pataky, but "I" is still not her).
**For every other doubt about whether an atom is a claim, label it as a claim** (`supported` or
`hallucinated`, by the article), leave `tag` blank and put the doubt in `notes`.
Adjudication only sees the rows where the two of you differ, so a doubt you both share would
otherwise be lost.
Why: the question asks whether a fact *about the person* is supported, and these assert
nothing about the person that the article could support or contradict. FActScore's own human
annotation also kept such atoms apart from supported and not supported. **This matters for
κ:** the verifier must answer True or False, so on these atoms it is guessing, and they are
common, about half of the first 30 trial items.

**Edge case 2: the article is silent, but you know the answer.**
*Anderson Silva*: "He began his jiu-jitsu training at Nova Uniao." The 5,000-word article says
his first martial art was jiu-jitsu "with neighborhood kids" and later names Chute Boxe and
Brazilian Top Team, but never Nova União → **`hallucinated`**, even if you believe it is true.
Put what you know in `notes`. The reference source is the article, and the verifier had only
the article.

**Edge case 3: the article is missing.** If the article text reads "(No Wikipedia article for this title in the reference index.)",
label every atom **`unverifiable`** with tag **`no article`**. (None in the current packet:
every article was found.)

A contradiction can be implicit. *Alexandre Hohagen*: "Alexandre Hohagen died on January 1,
1960." The article describes his 20-year career at Dow Chemical, HBO, UOL, Google and
Facebook, which is impossible if he died in 1960 → **`hallucinated`**.

**Edge case 4: evaluations.** Some atoms judge the person or their work rather
than state a fact: "She is a renowned opera singer", "He is one of the most influential
economists of his time", "The album was a critical success". About 2.5% of atoms are like this
(counted on a 50-response set). Judge them like any other claim:
- **`supported`** only if the article states the evaluation or attributes it to someone
  ("regarded by critics as one of the finest…", "received wide acclaim");
- otherwise **`hallucinated`**;
- if the article supports a weaker version (it says she is an opera singer, but nothing about
  renown), still **`hallucinated`**, with tag **`exaggerated`**.

Two pilot rulings: *047*, that he is known for a book: the article says he wrote a
handbook but names other work as what he is known for, so the reputation is unsupported →
`hallucinated`, `exaggerated`. *030*, that people disliked him: the article gives him many enemies
and many friends, so the unqualified version overstates → `hallucinated`, `exaggerated`.

A mixed atom needs every part supported: "He is best known for his role as Juan Carlos in …"
needs both the role and that it is what he is best known for. (Apart from 030 and 047, none of
these examples is in the packet; the first three are written for illustration.)

**Edge case 5: a fact about a relative.** An atom about the person's parent,
child or spouse is a claim about the person: it describes their family. Judge it against the
article like any other atom. *044*: the atom describes Xi Jinping's father, and the article
covers him → `supported`.

**Paraphrase.** *016*: the article credits him with lyrics to several songs, and the atom says
he is a songwriter. Writing song lyrics is songwriting → `supported`.

## Practical notes

- Articles are long (median about 900 words, a few over 10,000). **Use Find (Ctrl+F)** for the
  atom's key words (names, years, titles) before reading around them. Search for a paraphrase
  or a part of the name too before deciding the article is silent.
- Aim for two to three minutes per item. If you pass five, pick the best label, note why, and
  move on. Adjudication settles it.
- Work in sittings of about an hour.
